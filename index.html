Getting started

What is SOX IT Audit?

A structured primer for anyone new to the Sarbanes-Oxley Act and its IT implications.

Why SOX exists

The Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002) was enacted after major corporate fraud at Enron and WorldCom. It requires public companies to maintain and attest to reliable internal controls over financial reporting (ICFR). CEOs and CFOs personally certify financial statements under Sections 302 and 906.

Why IT is in scope

Financial data flows through IT systems. If the underlying systems — ERP, databases, access controls, change management — are unreliable, financial reports cannot be trusted. IT General Controls (ITGCs) are the foundational layer that make application controls trustworthy.

Section 404 — the core requirement

404(a): Management must assess and report on ICFR effectiveness annually. 404(b): External auditors (for accelerated filers) must independently attest to that assessment. ITGCs are tested as part of both obligations. Non-accelerated filers and EGCs are exempt from 404(b).

Key players

Management designs and operates controls. Internal Audit tests controls and provides assurance (and advisory). External Auditors (PCAOB-registered firms) independently attest. Audit Committee provides governance oversight. Each role has distinct responsibilities under IIA and PCAOB standards.

What can go wrong

Control weaknesses are classified as Control Deficiencies, Significant Deficiencies, or Material Weaknesses. A material weakness requires public disclosure and can trigger SEC enforcement. Roughly 20–25% of U.S. public companies disclose at least one material weakness annually.

2026 landscape

AI, cloud ERP, multi-cloud identity, and third-party supply chain risk have fundamentally expanded ITGC scope. New PCAOB standards (AS 1000, AS 1105, QC 1000 effective Dec 2026) raise evidence standards. SOX programs now cost $2.3M+ and 15,000+ hours on average per KPMG's 2025 survey.

IT General Controls

ITGC domains

The four core ITGC domains auditors test under SOX Section 404, each mapped to COSO components, COBIT domains, and PCAOB paragraphs.

LA · Logical Access

Access management

Controls ensuring only authorized users access financially significant systems, data, and reports — from provisioning to deprovisioning.

  • User provisioning & deprovisioning
  • Role-based access control (RBAC)
  • Privileged access management (PAM)
  • Periodic access reviews (user recertification)
  • Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
  • Segregation of duties (SoD) enforcement
  • Non-human identity (NHI) governance
  • Remote access controls

Change management

Controls ensuring all changes to financially significant applications, infrastructure, and configurations are authorized, tested, and documented before promotion to production.

  • Release management & version control
  • Patch management
  • Configuration management baseline

Computer operations

Controls over the day-to-day operation of systems supporting financial processes — ensuring jobs run, failures are detected, and data is protected.

  • Capacity monitoring
  • Network monitoring & alerting
  • Data integrity controls

Advisory & Assurance

Advisory vs. assurance — the critical distinction

Understanding the IIA's Three Lines model and where advisory ends and assurance begins is foundational to an effective SOX program.

The IIA's Three Lines Model (2020) replaces the older "Three Lines of Defense." The shift from "defense" to "lines" emphasizes collaboration and value creation, not just risk prevention.
First line

Management & control owners

  • Owns and operates ITGC controls
  • Performs self-assessments (CSA)
  • Responsible for remediation
  • Provides evidence to auditors
  • Certifies control effectiveness
Second line

Risk & compliance functions

  • SOX program management office
  • Risk framework oversight (COSO, COBIT)
  • Policy & procedure governance
  • Compliance monitoring
  • Control design advisory support
Third line

Internal audit

  • Independent assurance on ICFR
  • ITGC testing (TOC)
  • Deficiency identification & escalation
  • Advisory services (within independence limits)
  • Reliance by external auditors

Governing body (board/audit committee) sits above all three lines. It provides oversight of the entire governance structure, receives reports from Internal Audit, and approves the external auditor engagement under SEC audit committee independence rules (Section 301).

Walkthroughs

Required by PCAOB AS 2201.34. Trace one transaction end-to-end through each significant process to confirm design understanding. Not a test of operating effectiveness — a test of understanding. Identifies design gaps before testing begins.

AS 2201.34IIA 2110

Testing of design effectiveness

Does the control, if performed as described, prevent or detect a material misstatement? Design testing is typically done through inquiry, observation, and inspection of the control documentation — before operating effectiveness is tested.

AS 2201.42COSO Principle 10

Testing of operating effectiveness (TOE)

Has the control operated as designed throughout the period? TOE requires sample testing with appropriate sample sizes based on control frequency. High-frequency daily controls require larger samples than quarterly management review controls. PCAOB AS 2315 governs sampling.

AS 2201.44, AS 2315

Interim vs. year-end testing

Interim testing (Q1–Q3) allows early deficiency detection and time for remediation before year-end. If deficiencies are remediated and re-tested before year-end, auditors may be able to issue a clean opinion. Year-end testing confirms controls operated through the full period.

IPE — information produced by entity

Under updated AS 1105, system-generated reports used in controls must be validated for completeness and accuracy. Auditors now require stronger corroboration of IPE reliability. Spreadsheets used as controls require their own ITGC-level validation. This is among the top PCAOB inspection findings.

AS 1105.10A

Reliance on internal audit work

PCAOB AS 2201.16–.19 allows external auditors to rely on IA work for low-risk controls. To qualify, IA must be adequately supervised, competent, and objective. External auditors must re-perform a portion of IA's work to validate quality. Per KPMG's survey, 56% of external auditors test fewer controls than management.

Advisory services do not produce an opinion on control effectiveness. Internal Audit must document advisory engagements separately and manage independence impairment per IIA Standard 1130.

Control design consulting

Advising management on control design before controls are implemented. IA can recommend control structures without impairment, provided management retains decision-making authority. Reviewing control narratives and documenting design choices are common advisory activities.

SOX readiness assessments

For pre-IPO companies or post-acquisition integration, IA and external advisors assess control gaps against SOX requirements 12–18 months before first audit. Identifies scoping, design, and documentation gaps before external audit scrutiny begins.

Control rationalization

Reducing the total number of controls without increasing risk. Combines redundant controls, eliminates low-value controls, and re-scopes materiality thresholds. Typically reduces testing burden by 15–30% while maintaining coverage. An advisory deliverable — not an audit opinion.

Root cause analysis

When a control fails, advisory goes beyond identifying the failure to diagnosing systemic causes: lack of training, process design flaw, resource constraints, or technology gaps. Root cause analysis informs remediation strategy and prevents recurrence — a different deliverable from the audit finding itself.

ERP go-live control advisory

Pre-go-live advisory on ITGC control design for new ERP implementations (SAP S/4HANA, Oracle Fusion, Workday). Covers access control architecture, SoD rule sets, data migration controls, and cutover procedures. Differs fundamentally from post-go-live testing of those same controls.

Control owner training

Training control owners on what their control is designed to prevent, what evidence is required, and how to document performance. Reduces evidence gaps and control failures caused by lack of understanding — the most common root cause of ITGC deficiencies at first-time SOX filers.

1

Apply a top-down, risk-based scoping approach

Start with financial statement risks → identify significant accounts → map to processes → identify in-scope applications → scope ITGC domains. PCAOB AS 2201.22 and SEC guidance both require risk-based scoping. Over-scoping wastes resources; under-scoping creates audit exposure.

AS 2201.22COSO Principle 7
2

Separate advisory and assurance engagements formally

Document each type of engagement separately. IA teams that design a control and later test it create an independence impairment that must be disclosed to the audit committee (IIA Standard 1130.A1). External auditors cannot rely on IA work where independence is impaired.

IIA Std. 1130AS 2201.17
3

Test interim controls — not just year-end

Interim testing (Q1–Q3) identifies deficiencies while there is still time to remediate and re-test before year-end. PCAOB expects auditors to evaluate whether roll-forward procedures adequately cover the remaining period. Build a testing calendar with interim milestones, not a single Q4 crunch.

AS 2201.49
4

Validate all IPE used in key controls

Every system-generated report, data extract, or spreadsheet used in a key control must be validated for completeness and accuracy. Document the source, the parameters, and the validation procedure. This is the single most commonly cited deficiency in PCAOB inspection reports (2023–2025).

AS 1105.10A
5

Review vendor SOC 1 reports annually — not just at onboarding

If a vendor processes financially material data, obtain their SOC 1 Type II report annually. Evaluate complementary user entity controls (CUECs). Ensure your controls address every CUEC listed in the report. Gap in CUECs = gap in your ITGC coverage.

AS 2601COBIT APO10
6

Implement continuous monitoring between audit cycles

Auditors increasingly scrutinize whether management monitors controls year-round, not just before audit. Automated access review alerts, change management exception reports, and anomaly detection tools provide real-time evidence of operating effectiveness — reducing last-minute evidence scrambles.

COSO Principle 16COBIT MEA01
7

Classify deficiencies promptly and escalate appropriately

Not every control failure is a material weakness. Classify promptly using PCAOB AS 2201.62–.70 criteria. Evaluate compensating controls. Communicate significant deficiencies to the audit committee in writing before the fiscal year-end. Timeliness of classification and escalation is itself an audit finding area.

AS 2201.62–.70IIA Std. 2060
8

Align control documentation with how controls actually operate

Control narratives must reflect current practice — not legacy documentation. Auditors perform walkthroughs specifically to test whether controls operate as documented. Outdated RCMs and narratives are a leading cause of walkthrough findings and design deficiencies in PCAOB inspections.

AS 2201.34
SOX Section 201 prohibits external audit firms from performing certain non-audit services for their audit clients — including designing ITGC controls, implementing systems, or providing management consulting. These are hard legal prohibitions, not just best practices.

Management

Owns controls — neither advisory nor assurance. Responsible for design, operation, and documentation. Cannot assess its own controls — that's the auditor's role.

Section 302 certifier Section 404(a) responsible

Internal audit

Can perform both advisory AND assurance. Independence impairment occurs if IA designed a control it later tests. Must disclose impairments to the audit committee and note them in engagement records. External auditors reduce reliance on impaired IA work.

IIA Std. 1130 AS 2201.17

External auditor

Cannot (for audit clients): design/implement systems, provide bookkeeping, perform internal audit outsourcing, provide management consulting, or design ITGC controls. These are prohibited under SOX Section 201 and PCAOB Rule 3523.

SOX §201 PCAOB Rule 3523

The co-sourcing question

Co-sourcing (a firm supplements an in-house IA team) is permissible for external audit firms, provided the co-sourced staff is not from the same firm as the external auditor, or independence safeguards are documented. Full outsourcing of the IA function to the external auditor is prohibited for public companies. Many organizations use a non-audit advisory firm for IA co-sourcing and ITGC advisory, keeping the external auditor relationship structurally separate.

AI & Emerging Tech Controls

AI controls within ITGC scope

When AI influences financially material processes, it must be scoped into ITGC testing. PCAOB AS 2201 already covers AI-assisted controls — no new standard needed.

No authoritative ITGC framework for AI exists yet. The PCAOB's TIA Working Group identified AI audit documentation as a top strategic priority. Until formal guidance arrives, AS 2201's existing framework — change controls, access controls, computer operations, program development — applies directly to AI systems.

AI change management

AI models in ICFR must have the same change controls as any other financially significant application.

  • Model versioning and change log
  • Approval workflow for model updates
  • Separation of training and production environments
  • Rollback procedures for failed updates
  • Vendor model update notification controls
AS 2201 Para .28

AI access controls

Who can access the model, its training data, its outputs, and its configuration must be governed and tested.

  • Access to model configuration & hyperparameters
  • Access to training datasets (data poisoning risk)
  • API key governance for AI service accounts
  • Human review access for override decisions
  • Audit logging of model interactions
AS 2201 Para .26

AI computer operations

AI systems must be monitored for availability, accuracy drift, and anomalous output in financial processes.

  • Model performance monitoring (accuracy, drift)
  • Batch inference job scheduling & alerting
  • Output anomaly detection
  • Fallback procedures when model is unavailable
  • Data quality controls for model inputs
AS 2201 Para .27

AI program development

Initial deployment of an AI model into a financial process is equivalent to a system implementation and requires full SDLC controls.

  • Model selection & validation documentation
  • UAT with finance stakeholders
  • Bias & fairness testing
  • Model cards & risk documentation
  • Post-implementation review (60/90 days)
AS 2201 Para .29

Third-party AI as a service organization

When a company uses a third-party AI platform (e.g. an AI-powered revenue forecasting tool) in ICFR, that vendor may qualify as a service organization under AS 2601 and Auditing Interpretation AI 18. This means auditors need to obtain and evaluate a SOC 1 report from the vendor. The problem: standard SOC 1 Type II reports were not designed for AI systems. They do not address model drift, training data governance, foundation model dependencies, or adversarial input risks. If no SOC 1 is available, auditors must perform alternative procedures to gain comfort over the vendor controls.

Non-human identities (NHI)

Service accounts, bots, RPA agents, and API keys now carry the same access risk as human users. Audit NHI provisioning, privilege levels, and decommissioning with the same rigor as human access reviews.

RPA in ICFR

RPA bots performing financial reconciliations or journal postings are automated controls — they require change management, access controls, and monitoring just like ERP modules. Bot failure = control failure.

Cloud & SaaS ITGC

When ERP or financial systems are SaaS-hosted, traditional ITGC testing shifts to reviewing vendor SOC 1 reports and testing complementary user entity controls (CUECs). Organizations retain responsibility for access, change, and configuration controls in their SaaS environment.

IPO Readiness & Case Studies

SOX readiness for pre-IPO companies

Building a SOX-ready control environment from scratch requires 12–18 months of preparation. Most first-time filers underestimate the ITGC component significantly.

18-month readiness timeline

Month 1–3

Scoping & risk assessment

Identify significant accounts and disclosures. Map financial processes. Identify in-scope applications and IT systems. Perform preliminary materiality threshold analysis. Engage external advisors.

Month 4–6

Control design & documentation

Design ITGC control frameworks for each domain. Document control narratives and RCM. Identify and close design gaps. Establish SDLC and change management procedures. Implement access review cadence.

Month 7–9

Control implementation & training

Implement controls and begin operating. Train control owners on evidence requirements. Establish SOC 1 review process for key vendors. Begin tracking access review completion rates.

Month 10–12

Internal readiness testing

Perform first internal walkthrough of all significant processes. Conduct preliminary TOE testing. Identify and remediate deficiencies. Document remediation and re-test. Build deficiency log.

Month 13–15

External auditor onboarding

Engage PCAOB-registered external auditor. Perform integrated audit planning. Agree on testing approach, sample sizes, and evidence formats. Address auditor walkthroughs and design effectiveness reviews.

Month 16–18

Year-end testing & S-1/10-K readiness

Complete year-end TOE testing. Address deficiencies and obtain management remediation evidence. External auditor issues integrated audit opinion. CFO/CEO certification of Section 302 and 906. File with SEC.

PCAOB 2024–2026 standards

Every new and amended standard affecting ITGC and ICFR audits — with effective dates, key changes, and what auditors should be doing now.

✓ Active — Dec 17, 2024

AS 1000

General Responsibilities of the Auditor

Consolidates and modernizes foundational auditor principles: professional skepticism, due care, engagement quality review. Reaffirms that ICFR audit obligations apply with the same rigor as financial statement audits. Replaces multiple legacy foundational standards.

Effective: fiscal years ending on or after Dec 15, 2024
✓ Active — Dec 2024

AS 1105 Amendment

Audit Evidence — Para .10A

New requirement to evaluate reliability of company-provided external electronic information (IPE). Auditors must understand the source, receiving process, and maintenance. Requires separate testing of reliability in many circumstances. Heavily inspected in 2025–2026 cycle.

Effective: fiscal years beginning on or after Dec 15, 2025
✓ Active — Dec 2024

AS 2301 Amendment

Technology-Assisted Data Analysis

Specifies auditor responsibilities when using technology-assisted analytical tools including AI, data analytics, and automated testing tools. Addresses 100% population testing and the evidentiary standards that apply.

Effective: fiscal years beginning on or after Dec 15, 2025
↗ Upcoming — Dec 15, 2026

AS 2201 Amendment

ICFR Integrated Audit

Amendments to paragraph .09 and new paragraph .99 strengthen the link between ICFR control testing and financial statement audit conclusions. Clarifies how top-down risk-based scoping applies to entity-level controls. Effective Dec 15, 2026 per PCAOB Release 2024-005.

Effective: fiscal years beginning on or after Dec 15, 2026
↗ Upcoming — Dec 15, 2026

AS 2101 Amendment

Audit Planning

Updated planning requirements clarifying how auditors determine locations and business units for procedures. Reinforces use of IA and other specialists in planning decisions. Aligned with new AS 2201 scoping requirements.

Effective: fiscal years beginning on or after Dec 15, 2026
⚠ Critical — Dec 15, 2026

QC 1000

Quality Control Standard

New firm-level quality control regime. Requires firms to design, implement, and operate a quality control system. Form QC reports due November 30, 2027. Delayed one year from 2025 effective date due to implementation challenges. Aligns with AICPA SQMS No. 1.

Form QC filing due: Nov 30, 2027
✓ Active — Aug 2024

AS 2315 Amendment

Audit Sampling

Updates align sampling requirements with the broader focus on sufficient appropriate audit evidence. Clarifies documentation basis for sample size decisions. Particularly relevant to ITGC TOE testing where sample size methodology is frequently inspected.

Effective: fiscal years ending on or after Dec 15, 2024
✓ Active

AS 2601 / AI 18

Service Organizations

Governs how auditors evaluate service organizations (including SaaS, cloud ERP, and now AI vendors) that affect ICFR. AI 18 extends coverage to subservice organizations — critical when AI platforms rely on third-party LLM APIs.

Ongoing — applies to all current audits
